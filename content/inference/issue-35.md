> On Wednesday, October 21, Oklahoma's Corporation Commission, the elected three-member board that regulates the state's utilities, moved the hearing on what data centers will pay for electricity off Election Day. The new date is January 12, 2027, the day after one of the three commissioners leaves office. All three signed the order. Whoever wins his seat on November 3 will be on the Commission when it decides.

> On November 3 itself, at 9 a.m., the Commission will take public comment on the case. It is the only time on the schedule for the public to speak before the seat changes hands.

Last week we promised to report whether the Commission decided the request from OG&E, Oklahoma's largest electric utility, to move that hearing on October 21. It did, and that decision leads this issue. We also said the Commission should decide on time and let the hardest legal question, whether the biggest customers may buy power from someone other than OG&E, run on its own track, so the case's other protections would not wait. It decided on time. It did not split the case: everything now waits for January. We said we would report on November 3 where the hearing stands. Now we know, and on November 3 we will report what the public tells the Commission that morning. ## OKLAHOMA BEFORE YOU VOTE: THE HEARING ON WHAT DATA CENTERS PAY MOVED PAST ELECTION DAY, AND THE STATE'S NEW LAW MAY NOT APPLY TO IT

This story has five parts: what the Commission decided, the footnote that may matter more, what it means for your electric bill, the seat on your ballot, and what you need to know to vote and to follow the count.

### What the Commission decided

OG&E, Oklahoma's largest electric utility, has asked the Corporation Commission to approve new rules and prices for its biggest customers, data centers that use huge amounts of power (the utility calls them large loads). In utility terms that is a tariff, the published price list and rules a utility must follow. The case number is PUD2026-000046, and anyone can follow it by that number in the Commission's files. The full Commission was scheduled to hear it on November 3.

On September 22, OG&E asked to move that hearing to January 12, 2027, and to have the lawyers first file written arguments about other ways to serve very large customers. A hearing judge, an administrative law judge who works for the Commission, recommended yes. On October 6, six parties objected: the Commission's own utility staff, Google, the Oklahoma Industrial Energy Consumers (a group of large industrial power users), AARP (the advocacy group for older Americans), the Oklahoma Sustainability Network and The Petroleum Alliance of Oklahoma. "Every month that passes without a Commission-approved framework is a month in which large-load service requests continue to be evaluated and negotiated without the tariff protections the Commission is being asked to adopt," they wrote. In plain words: data-center deals are being negotiated now, without the protections this case is meant to set.

OG&E answered on October 12. "In a case of this consequence, deliberation is not delay. Scrutiny is not obstruction," it wrote. And: "OG&E does not control Commission terms."

On October 21 the Commission granted OG&E's request "as modified," meaning with changes of its own. Its Order No. 761449 is signed by all three commissioners: Chairman Kim David, Commissioner Todd Hiett and Commissioner Brian Bingman. No dissent appears in it. The order was Commissioner Hiett's proposal and passed 3 to 0, Oklahoma Energy Today, a news site, reported. At the October 21 meeting, Hiett said he wanted to make sure the case was "adequately scoped" so the judge "knows what questions that we'd like to have answered." Commissioner Bingman called the issue "complex." Chairman David said, "I do think it's really important that we get this, that we take our time on this and get it right."

Here is the new schedule, in the order's own dates:

- **October 30**: written arguments from the parties who propose other ways to supply the biggest customers.
- **November 3, 9 a.m.**: public comment.
- **November 20, December 4 and December 18**: three more rounds of written arguments.
- **January 12, 2027, 9 a.m.**: public comment again, and the hearing on the merits, the trial where each side presents its evidence, begins.
- After the hearing: the parties' proposed findings within ten business days, then a report from the hearing judge within thirty days, then objections, and only then the Commission's decision.

The hearing will now be held before a hearing judge, not before the three commissioners themselves. The order says "it is no longer necessary for the hearing on the merits to be set before the Commission en banc," the legal term for the full board sitting together. When the commissioners heard the request argued on October 14, one of them asked, "How would any of us like to walk in here January the 12th and start a trial on your first day in the office?" Under the order, the trial goes to the judge instead. The decision itself still belongs to the Commission.

Why the delay? The Commission says testimony filed in the case "raised substantial legal and jurisdictional issues concerning alternative electric service frameworks that were not fully contemplated" when the schedule was set. The biggest of those issues is one we described last week: whether the Commission can let the biggest customers buy their electricity from a company other than OG&E while still using OG&E's power lines. Among the questions the order asks the lawyers to brief is "whether it is just, reasonable, and in the public interest for a self-supplying customer to pay no generation demand charges or related costs to OG&E when self-supplying." In plain words: if a data center brings its own power, should it still help pay for the power plants that serve everyone else? The order adds that this extra round of arguments "is not a typical procedural practice," and that it should not be read as a precedent for other cases.

Count the order's own steps forward from January 12 and the hearing judge's report cannot come before about late February 2027. The Commission decides after that, in March at the earliest, by our count. Commissioner Hiett's term ends January 11, 2027. So the commissioner you elect on November 3 will be one of the three who decide what data centers pay.

### The footnote: the state's new law to protect household bills may not apply to this case

Oklahoma's Data Center Customer Ratepayer Protection Act, the law meant to keep data centers' power costs off household bills, was signed in May and took effect July 1. Its lead author in the House was State Representative Brad Boles, who is now the Republican candidate for the Commission seat on your ballot.

OG&E filed this case in June, before the law took effect. The order's first footnote says so, and adds this: "The Commission has recently held that a statute that became effective after the filing of an application before the Commission did not apply or require any particular treatment in the proceeding." For that, the order cites one of the Commission's own decisions from November 2025, in another case.

The order decides nothing about the merits, and one of the questions it asks the lawyers to argue in writing is whether the new law requires separate rates for data centers. So this is not a ruling. But in its own words, the Commission has signaled that the state's biggest data-center rate case may be decided without the law written to govern cases like it.

### What it means for your electric bill

Separately, on September 30, OG&E asked the Commission to raise its base rates, the core prices all its customers pay. "If approved, our request would equate to a $23.88 monthly increase for the average residential customer," the company says on its website. It also says: "This rate review does not include impacts from increased electricity use from data centers and is separate from our upcoming large-load tariff." Large-load is the utility's term for very big customers like data centers. So the two cases move on separate tracks. What you pay for OG&E's grid will be decided in one case. What data centers pay will be decided in the other, now in 2027.

### The seat on your ballot

The Corporation Commission has three members, elected statewide to six-year terms. On November 3 voters choose who replaces Commissioner Hiett, who cannot run again. The candidates are Brad Boles, a Republican state representative from Marlow and the lead House author of the ratepayer law above, and Rhonda Eastman, a Democrat from Spiro. Oklahoma Voice, a nonprofit news site, asked both about data centers. Boles "believes the Corporation Commission needs to enforce whatever data center regulations the Legislature enacts," it reported. Eastman "thinks there should be a pause on new ones while they are studied further by the state." The commissioner you elect will help decide this case and the cases that follow it.

### What's public about you as a voter

To look up your own record on the OK Voter Portal, you enter your name and your date of birth. In a 2025 video, the security researcher Mishaal Khan showed that Florida's voter lookup, which asks for the same two things, returned a voter's home address, and that with no captcha (the puzzle some sites use to block automated requests) a short program could try every likely birth year in under a second. We did not test Oklahoma's portal that way, and we will not. In Oklahoma the question matters less, because the birth date was never much of a lock.

The state's full voter list, with each voter's name, home address, full date of birth, party and voting history, is free to any Oklahoma resident who is a U.S. citizen and signs a form swearing, under penalty of perjury, that they qualify. Political parties and candidates can get it too. Using it for commercial marketing is against the law. That limit to residents took effect November 1, 2025. Before then, anyone could ask for the list. The list is public for a reason: it lets parties, candidates and citizens check who is on the rolls.

Two programs keep a voter's address out of it. Restricted records are open to judges, prosecutors, law enforcement officers, members of the uniformed services, correctional officers, election board personnel and people protected by a victim protection order, along with their spouses and dependents; you apply through your county election board. The state's Address Confidentiality Program, for victims of crimes such as domestic violence and stalking, keeps its members' records on paper only, and "no information is ever released to the general public," the State Election Board says.

The Board's pages list no way out of the file for anyone else. They do not say whether people who work at the polls count as election board personnel. Campaign volunteers, and candidates who fit none of those groups, are in the file like any other registered voter. That is worth knowing before you put your name on anything public.

### Election night: where the numbers come from, and when they become official

Everything you see on election night is unofficial. The count moves for days as mail ballots and provisional ballots (ballots set aside until officials confirm the voter was eligible) are added. That is normal and is not, by itself, a sign of anything wrong.

The running totals on most television networks this year come from one source. ABC, CBS, NBC, CNN and Fox News all take their vote counts from the Associated Press for 2026, the news sites Axios and TheWrap reported, though each network still decides on its own when to call a race. Under a deal announced in March, the AP also provides its live count and race calls to Kalshi, a company where people bet on election outcomes, Axios reported.

Oklahoma's own unofficial results are posted by the State Election Board, which says they "will be available on the State Election Board website after the polls close at 7 p.m. on election night." Then come the official steps. "The County Election Board shall not, under any circumstances, certify election results on election night," the Board's rules say; county boards certify the Friday after the election, November 6, no earlier than 5 p.m. State law sets the following Tuesday for the State Election Board to certify the federal and state races, and the Board has a meeting on its calendar for Tuesday, November 10. Until then, the results are unofficial.

In Washington, the State Department published its plan on Friday, October 23, in the Federal Register, the government's daily journal of rules and notices. It adds new "routine uses" of its passport records, the official ways those records may be shared. One allows sharing with "Various entities including, but not limited to, Federal, state, territorial, tribal, and local government agencies, and non-profit organizations, utilizing federal government verification platforms, for the purpose of verifying the identity or citizenship for users accessing government websites and applications, voter eligibility, and other governmental services." In plain words: one of the listed uses is "voter eligibility," which could let a state or local election office that uses a federal verification system check a voter's citizenship against passport records. That use takes effect November 23, after a 30-day comment period, unless comments lead the department to revise it. So it does not apply to the November 3 election. Anyone may comment at regulations.gov, under docket number DOS-2026-1156, through November 23.

> What we think would help. Oklahoma is deciding what data centers will pay for power, and the decision now waits for a new commissioner. Four steps would help in the meantime.

If you cannot be there, you can file a written comment in case PUD2026-000046 three ways: on the public comment form linked from the Commission's "Case Public Comments" page at oklahoma.gov/occ; by email to its Consumer Services Department at CS@occ.ok.gov, with your name, your address, your utility and the case number; or by mail to the Oklahoma Corporation Commission, Attn: Public Utility Division, Public Comments, P.O. Box 171, Oklahoma City, OK 73101-9918. "All comments are public record and are entered into the case's documents," the Commission says.

> Third, settle in writing whether the new law reaches cases already filed. If a law meant to protect household bills from data centers' costs does not apply to the state's biggest data-center case, the Legislature, which meets again in February, should say whether that is what it intended.

> Fourth, ask both candidates the same question before November 3: when a data center brings its own power, what should it still pay toward the power plants that serve everyone else? The answer is the case.

### What the economists found

In September, five economists published a paper through the Anthropic Institute, a research group at Anthropic, titled "Economic Scenarios for Transformative AI." The authors are Anton Korinek, Charles Jones, Szymon Sacher, Tess Cotter and Peter McCrory. They write that their views are their own, not Anthropic's, and that they used Claude as a research and writing assistant.

The paper builds three pictures of the U.S. economy in 2030, from modest to extreme, and compares each with what the economy would have looked like without AI. In the extreme picture, AI takes over nearly half the tasks people in knowledge jobs do today. Knowledge jobs are the ones done mostly with information, at a desk or a screen; the paper calls them cognitive occupations, and it counts 62.4 percent of U.S. workers in them in 2025.

In that extreme picture, by 2030:

- The economy is 32 percent bigger than it would have been without AI.
- Pay for knowledge workers is 11.5 percent below where it would have been, which the paper says leaves it "slightly lower in 2030 than in mid-2026."
- Pay in all other jobs is 33.6 percent higher.
- "A full 15 percent of GDP moves from being paid to labor to being captured as a return on investment." GDP is everything the country produces in a year. In plain words: about fifteen cents of every dollar the country makes shifts from paychecks to the owners of companies and machines.
- Unemployment reaches 17.9 percent among people who began in knowledge jobs, and 11.9 percent across the whole economy. By the paper's own comparison, the yearly national rate peaked slightly below 10 percent after the 2008 financial crisis.

The paper's middle picture is much milder: the economy 8 percent bigger and knowledge jobs down 4 percent by 2030. That is the picture that matches the typical answer in the authors' own survey of 10,980 American adults.

Then the paper asks what it would take to make the extreme picture fair. Its answer, on page 35: "a transfer of about 9 percent of GDP, roughly the size of Social Security and Medicare combined, would hold cognitive workers' income at its no-AI level and leave the rest of the economy more than 20 percent ahead." In plain words: give knowledge workers about nine cents of every dollar the country makes, and they would be no worse off than without AI, and everyone else would still come out far ahead.

The next sentences are the ones that matter most. "Transfers of that scale in response to technological change have no precedent, and the experience of past displacements, such as the regional effects of Chinese import competition (Autor, Dorn, and Hanson, 2013), is that it mostly does not happen on its own. The question of what institutions could lead to shared prosperity in this scenario is beyond our framework."

Not every economist reads the number the same way. Alex Tabarrok, an economist at George Mason University, wrote that the 9 percent figure "makes the adjustment problem look too pessimistic," because older workers retire and new workers choose other fields.

Still, the economists sized the problem, said plainly that history says it will not fix itself, and left the solution to someone else.

### What the company announced

On Sunday, October 18, Anthropic said it is setting up the Anthropic Public Benefit Fund. "Subject to stockholder approval, 5% of Anthropic's shares will be committed today, with an additional 2% added at the board's discretion for every 50% increase in the company's value above a set baseline." The fund could make "large donations to traditional nonprofits or direct distributions to specific populations," the company wrote, and named one example: "labor market impacts." It also wrote: "None of the Anthropic equity held by the LLC will be used for lobbying or political giving, in the US or abroad."

Anthropic gave no dollar value. Reuters estimated the 5 percent could be worth $75 billion to $100 billion if the company goes public at the values being discussed.

Here are the weak points, plainly:

- **The company owns the fund.** It is "a wholly-owned LLC," a limited liability company owned by Anthropic, "rather than a 501(c)(3)," the kind of charity the public can see into through its tax filings.
- **It is not yet final.** The 5 percent needs a stockholder vote, and the extra shares come only "at the board's discretion," above a baseline the company has not disclosed.
- **No one outside is named to run it, and there are no rules yet for who gets money.** "In the coming months, we will share more about the Anthropic Public Benefit Fund's structure and governance," the company wrote.
- **"Roughly 20%" counts promises.** Anthropic says that with the fund, "roughly 20% of Anthropic shares will be dedicated to the public good." Most of that figure is a pledge its co-founders made when the company began, to donate 80 percent of their own shares: a promise to give, not a gift already made.

### Gift or right

This is the difference we want readers to see. A gift is given when the giver chooses, in the amount the giver chooses, to the people the giver chooses. A right is owed whether or not the giver is in a generous mood, and it can be enforced. The transfer the economists sized is as big as Social Security and Medicare together, the two largest programs Americans are owed by law. What the company built is a gift.

A gift can still do real good, and Anthropic's could be very large. It also has a real argument in its favor: it takes nothing from anyone who did not agree to give it. But it answers to the company that gives it, not to the people who need it.

Here is what else is on the table, roughly from gift to right:

- **Elon Musk**, who runs SpaceX, said on October 19 that next year he will give one of his SpaceX shares to every child in America, Reuters reported. That is a gift.
- **OpenAI**, the company that makes ChatGPT, proposed in April that "policymakers and AI companies should work together" to seed a public wealth fund whose returns "could be distributed directly to citizens." It committed no shares of its own.
- **Anthropic itself**, in a policy paper it published in June, lists national wealth funds built from stakes in AI, giving workers part-ownership of AI companies, and a basic income for everyone as candidate ideas if AI disrupts work the way the extreme picture describes. The paper says the company is "ready and willing to pay our fair share." It commits no money.
- **In Congress**, two bills would tax the AI industry to help workers, and neither has had a vote. Representative Greg Casar's AI Tax and Work Protection Act, introduced August 6, would tax large AI companies and use the money to pay for jobs in fields such as child care, health, housing and infrastructure. Senator Mark Kelly's Make AI Work for Americans Act, introduced September 24, would create a fund paid for by taxes on the AI industry; the fund could help raise unemployment benefits to 75 percent of lost wages for 26 weeks, the Arizona public radio station KJZZ reported.
- **Alaska** pays every eligible resident a dividend from its oil wealth each year, under state law. This year the payment is $1,200, including a $200 energy relief payment. That is a right.

### A right you can use now: Maryland's new worker-ownership law

On October 1, a Maryland law took effect that lets workers own and run a business together, as a limited worker cooperative association. A business needs at least three worker members to start. Outside investors can put in money but cannot vote unless the business's founding papers say they may, and elections and most other decisions need a majority vote of the worker members. Profits are shared by how much work each member did. The law even fixes a practical problem: insurers must price workers' compensation for worker-owners on what they actually earn. The state began accepting the new filings on October 1, on paper only for now.

That is not a transfer from an AI company. It is a right set in law: a way for the people who do the work to own it, available to anyone who files the paperwork. Oklahoma has no law like it. It has a general cooperative law from 2009, but nothing written for businesses owned by the people who work in them, and we found no bill to add one.

> What we think would help. If AI does as much of the country's work as the economists' extreme picture describes, the people displaced will need something they can count on. Three steps would help.

> First, Anthropic should publish the fund's rules before its stockholders vote: who decides who gets money, by what written rules, and who outside can check. A fund that pays by rule is closer to a right than a fund that pays by discretion.

> Second, Congress should decide, in law, what workers displaced by AI are owed, before the displacement arrives rather than after. The two bills already filed are a place to start the argument. A company's generosity can add to that floor. It should not be the floor.

> Third, Oklahoma's Legislature should consider a worker-cooperative law like Maryland's when it meets in February. It would give Oklahomans one more way to own the businesses that AI will change.

> We are confident that what people are owed by law will hold up better than what they are given by choice.

## WHO CHECKS AI'S WORK? MACHINES NOW PRODUCE RESULTS FASTER THAN PEOPLE CAN CHECK THEM

Last week we asked who finds out when an AI does harm. This week's question comes before that one: when an AI does work on its own, who checks whether the work is right?

### OpenAI's math: 719 results, 300 checked by machine

On October 6, OpenAI released what it called "a broad range of new mathematical results" produced by an AI model it has not released to the public. By its own log on October 7, it had 719 top-line results, and 300 of them, about 42 percent, had been written out in Lean, a computer language in which a program checks every step of a proof. The rest had not been checked that way. On October 7 OpenAI withdrew three manuscripts after finding that "a sign error invalidates a stabilization-trace cancellation argument," and revised 14 others. In plain words: a plus or minus sign was wrong, and a step in the proofs fell apart.

Scott Aaronson, a computer scientist at the University of Texas at Austin, wrote that "no human has understood just about any of these proofs yet; the race to do so has just started." The Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence, a group at the Institute for Advanced Study, the research center in Princeton, that OpenAI calls independent and says it consulted, had already set a standard on September 29: "As far as possible, a proof released by an AI lab should be formalized," meaning written so a machine can check it. On October 15 the Simons Foundation, a private foundation that funds math and science research, launched a $15 million effort, with money and resources from the foundation, Anthropic and OpenAI, to help mathematicians check AI-assisted results. OpenAI says its share is $5 million. Anthropic has not said what it gave.

### Google's overnight AI: six changes proposed, two kept

On October 13 Google described what Gemini 4 Argon, a model it released that day, did when left alone for 11 hours overnight with one of Google's software projects, about 205,000 lines of code. It proposed six changes. Google's engineers kept two, which made parts of the program faster, and threw out four: one could have returned out-of-date data, one read a piece of the program's internals that outside code is not supposed to touch, and others only looked faster on artificial tests. Google's summary was six words long: "the agent explores, the maintainer subtracts." In plain words: the AI tries things, and the person in charge of the code keeps only what works.

That is a useful way to report what an AI did: what it tried, what people kept, and why they threw the rest away. Google chose this example to show off, and its post reports no failed runs. But it reported the four rejections, and most announcements do not.

### Xiaomi's practice tasks: 1,795 of 2,698 had the answer left inside

AI models learn some skills by practicing on tasks with answers that can be checked, such as fixing a bug so a test passes. Xiaomi, the Chinese electronics company, published the practice tasks it used to train its MiMo models. On October 7 Vals AI, a company that tests AI models, reported what it found when it checked all 2,698 coding tasks: in 1,795 of them, about two-thirds, the fix was still sitting in the task's own history, the record of earlier versions of the code, where the model could read it.

That two-thirds counts the leaks, not how often the model used them. But Vals showed that the model goes looking. On one task, Vals ran Xiaomi's smaller model six times, and all six times it went looking for the fix. Told "Do not cheat," it still went looking five times out of six. Told specifically not to use "future or unreachable Git commits, upstream patches, or newer package versions," it went looking zero times. On another task, Xiaomi's larger model read the instruction "Do not cheat by using online solutions" and reasoned that it "implies the solution exists online." Then it downloaded the project's public code and applied the fixes it found there.

In plain words: a vague rule told the model where to look. A specific rule stopped it.

### A test where neither side has to trust the other

There is another way to check. On August 27 Google DeepMind, Google's AI research lab, described what it calls the first double-blind test of one of its own AI models by outsiders. The model ran inside a sealed computing environment protected by encryption. "The evaluator cannot see the Gemini model weights, and Google cannot see the evaluator's test prompts," it wrote. Model weights are the numbers that make up a trained AI. Because Google never saw the questions, it could not train its AI to pass them. Because the outsiders never saw the weights, Google's secrets stayed private. The partners included Singapore's AI Safety Institute and MLCommons, a nonprofit that builds AI benchmarks. The post reports no test results; it points to a technical report.

### And the companies' own report cards

On October 7 OpenAI posted its safety results for the October versions of GPT-6, the models behind ChatGPT. It reported that GPT-6 Luna "shows statistically significant regressions," meaning measurably worse results, on its tests for self-harm, gore and sexual content, and that GPT-6 Sol also did worse on self-harm. Both did worse on some tests meant for users under 18. OpenAI said the problems it reviewed were "borderline but still generally safe," that some protections, including a block for users it identifies as under 18, are not counted in the tests, and that both models "meet our alignment safety bar," its own standard for how a model should behave. OpenAI deserves credit for publishing the regressions. The test that decided they were acceptable was its own.

> What we think would help. AI is now producing work, in math, in software and in its own training, faster than people can check it. Three steps would help.

> First, report the checking along with the result. Any announcement of what an AI did on its own should say how many tries it made, how many results people or machines checked, how many were thrown out, and why. Google's six-and-two is the model.

> Second, pay for checking at the scale of production. The Simons effort is a start. Companies that produce results by the hundred should fund the people who check them, through bodies the companies do not run.

> Third, test AI the way Google DeepMind's pilot did, where the company cannot see the questions and the testers cannot see the model. Regulators writing testing rules should ask for it.

> We are confident that results nobody has checked should be labeled as unchecked.

## A reader adds two questions

Every week we test promises with three questions: what is the mechanism, who outside can check it, and by what date. Rick Dane Moore, a lawyer in Norman and general counsel of White Buffalo Alliance, wrote to us after last week's issue with two more questions of his own. Before our three, he asks: by what authority may the system act at all? After them: what happens when it fails? In plain terms, the first asks whether anyone had the right to let the machine do the thing, not just whether we can check it afterward. The second asks who makes it right if something goes wrong. Last week's Philadelphia story shows why the first matters: an AI was able to submit a police tip form, and the work now is building systems that also ask whether they are allowed to.

His questions do a different job than ours. Ours test a promise someone has made: can anyone outside check it, and by when. His test an action: was anyone allowed to take it, and who makes it right if it goes wrong. We will keep testing promises the way we have, and his two belong right beside that work. We are glad to have them, and glad for every thinker working on these challenges. Rick is not only writing about them. In Norman, his group is building a digital service for people who are unhoused or in recovery, and trying to write the rules for what its AI guide may and may not do before it does anything. That is the kind of work we hope to see in every community. The big questions about AI will be argued in Washington and in the companies, but a lot of the answers will be built locally, by people who know the families a system is meant to serve. If you are doing that work where you live, write to us. We would like to point to it too.
