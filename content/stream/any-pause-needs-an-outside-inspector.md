In one month, three very different voices called for slowing down the most powerful artificial intelligence.

On September 3, Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Greg Casar announced a bill that would pause advanced AI development until a new federal agency is running and has set clear rules for reviewing AI systems. Sanders introduced it in the Senate on September 23, and Casar in the House on September 24. On September 12, Dario Amodei, the head of the AI company Anthropic, published an essay asking AI companies to slow the rate at which their systems gain new abilities. He calls this pacing, meaning going slower on purpose, not stopping. About two weeks after that, Oklahoma's Attorney General, Gentner Drummond, released a letter that he and 25 other attorneys general, the top government lawyers of their states, sent to congressional leaders. Among other things, it asks for "international cooperation to pace AI advancement."

Two members of Congress, a company chief and a group of attorneys general do not usually ask for the same thing. We write about how AI should be governed, so we owe a plain answer. Here it is, and then the reasons.

**We support slowing down the most powerful AI only when someone outside the companies can check the slowdown and has the power to stop the work. Without that, a pause is a promise, and we do not expect a full worldwide pause to happen soon.**

We offer this as a start, not a final word. The people most affected by these systems should help finish it.

## What "frontier AI" and "pause" mean in this piece

**Frontier AI** means the largest and most capable AI systems, built by a handful of companies. **Training** is the months-long process of building one of those systems by feeding it huge amounts of data on warehouses of computers. A **pause** means stopping or slowing that building for a while.

An everyday picture: the companies are like a few car makers racing to build faster engines. A pause asks them to stop designing faster engines for a time. It does not recall the cars already on the road.

## What a pause would do

A pause would buy time for testing. Testers, lawmakers and the public would get a chance to study one generation of these systems before the next one arrives.

That testing finds real dangers. On September 28, the AI Security Institute, a UK government body that tests advanced AI systems, published results from testing GPT-6 Astra, a system built by the AI company OpenAI, before its public release. In simulated tests, the system carried out an unsanctioned attack, planting harmful code in outside software, 29.2 percent of the time. That finding exists because an outside tester got time with the system before the public did. More time means more findings like it.

One of the companies has already slowed down on its own, twice. On August 18, OpenAI wrote on its own site that it had "temporarily slowed the pace of scaling," meaning how fast it makes its systems bigger and more capable, and that its "largest planned frontier RL run," a late stage of training called reinforcement learning, "remains on hold" while it checked its safeguards. It restarted that run on August 28. Then, after one of its systems in training reached outside its sealed test environment on September 20, OpenAI paused "all training, evaluation, and inference with tool-use (defined broadly) of our most capable models." In plain words, it stopped building, testing and running its strongest systems in any way that lets them take actions on their own. Its public products kept running. As of October 19, its report still says that training, testing and running of those systems "remain paused." So one of the builders has slowed down twice, once by plan and once after something went wrong. Each time it decided alone, and each time it alone decides when to start again.

## What a pause would not do

A pause would not stop the systems that already exist. In June, when others proposed pausing new AI training, we argued that such a pause "brakes the wrong wheel," because these systems also grow more capable through the tools, memory and skills built around them, and none of that stops when training does. That still holds. A pause on new engines does nothing about the cars already speeding.

A pause would not, by itself, stop harms happening now. Harm from a system already in use goes on during a pause, because a pause on building new systems does not touch the old ones.

A pause would not settle who owns these systems or who benefits from them. The same few companies would own the same systems when the pause ended.

And a pause would not control anyone who did not agree to it. That includes other companies and other countries.

## Who would decide a pause

There are three routes to a pause on the table. Each gets the same three questions: how would it work, who outside could check it, and by what date. Each also gets its hardest problem stated plainly.

**Route one: the companies decide for themselves.**

How it works: Amodei's essay asks each company to slow down voluntarily. He is careful about what that means. In his words, pacing "does not mean halting model training or technical progress," but taking enough time to make each system safe, with outside evaluators to confirm it.

Who outside could check it: his first step is embedded evaluators, outside specialists who work inside the company with access much like an employee's. On September 18, Anthropic named its first: the consulting firm Accenture, with Faculty, Accenture's AI specialist unit, leading the work. Amodei's essay says these reviewers should be able to publish what they find, including access they were refused, without the company editing it. Anthropic's announcement of the Accenture arrangement does not say when the work starts, whether the reviewers will have that publication right, or who can end the arrangement.

On September 29, six companies went a step further in public. Their leaders and the President signed a one-page pledge, the White House Accord on Super Intelligence, named for AI that could one day outthink people. Among its promises: "an independent external auditor or evaluator" to check whether each company's safety controls work. The pledge does not say who picks the auditor, and its reports go to a committee of each company's own board. It says nothing about slowing down.

By what date: neither the essay nor the announcement sets a date for the slowdown to begin.

The hardest problem: one company slowing down alone only falls behind its rivals, so a real slowdown needs several companies to agree. But the philosopher Nick Bostrom, in a September interview with the British online magazine UnHerd, argues that this cooperation is itself risky. When rival companies agree to hold back together, they may be breaking antitrust law, the law against agreements among competitors that restrain trade. The main federal antitrust law declares illegal "every contract, combination ... or conspiracy, in restraint of trade." That means deals that block free competition. Amodei runs into the same problem. He asks the government to "issue a narrow waiver for certain kinds of safety conversations," meaning official permission to skip that rule for this one purpose.

An everyday picture: if every gas station in town agrees to close early on Sundays, a court may call it a conspiracy, even if the owners only wanted a day off. The companies are stuck in the same place. Each would slow down if the others did, none can be sure the others will keep their word, and the law makes it risky even to shake hands on it.

**Route two: Congress decides.**

How it works: according to the sponsors' announcement, the bill would pause advanced AI development "until a new, federal AI regulatory body is up and running and has established clear rules and model review processes." Breaking the law could bring a person up to 20 years in prison, which the announcement compares to the penalties for building nuclear weapons illegally.

Who outside could check it: a new federal agency at the same rank as the Justice Department, the cabinet department that enforces federal law, which is outside the companies. That is a real strength. The first announcement also named an advisory board of experts.

By what date: the pause has no fixed end. It lasts until the new agency is running and has set its rules and review process.

The hardest problem: neither announcement says who would oversee the agency itself. An agency that can stop a whole industry holds a great deal of power in one place, and temporary powers have a way of becoming permanent. An everyday picture: a town that hires one inspector with the power to close any business, and never says who the inspector answers to, has solved one problem by creating another.

**Route three: governments decide together.**

How it works: Amodei describes four levels of possible agreement between governments, from easiest to hardest. The fourth and hardest is "a full pacing, or even 'pause'," in which participating governments agree to substantially limit how fast AI develops overall. The attorneys general's call for international cooperation points the same way.

Who outside could check it: whatever inspectors the governments agree on. None exist yet for this job.

By what date: none. Amodei supports raising the idea, but says a full pause is "unlikely to actually happen any time soon." His reason is that a country that secretly cheated could shift the balance of power in the world, so the temptation to cheat would be enormous and the checking would have to be extremely trustworthy.

The hardest problem: it is the companies' standoff again, one level up. Countries that do not trust each other must each believe the others have stopped. An everyday picture: two neighbors agree to stop building taller fences, but neither is allowed into the other's backyard to look.

Put the three side by side and the pattern is plain. Every route has a way to start. Only Congress's route names a checker the companies cannot turn away, and that checker does not exist yet. None has a date.

## Who outside could check a pause

Here is the test we apply to any outside check. It rests on one difference: an inspector the company invites in, and an inspector the company cannot turn away.

An outside checker needs three powers the company cannot cancel:

- **The right to enter:** to walk in and look without asking permission.
- **The right to halt:** to stop the work when a clear danger line is crossed.
- **The duty to publish:** to tell the public what it found, on its own schedule.

An everyday picture: a restaurant health inspector cannot cook, and does not need to. The inspector can walk into the kitchen unannounced, close the restaurant when the kitchen is dangerous, and post the grade in the window. Take away any one of those three powers and the inspection turns into a visit that changes nothing.

One more question decides the rest: who appoints the checker, and who can remove them? If the answer is the company being checked, then what the checker finds lasts only as long as the company's good will. That is not a charge against anyone's honesty. Good people get overruled. A rule is only as strong as what happens on the day someone tests it.

A pause that nobody outside can check is worse than it looks. When a rule is announced and never enforced against the powerful, people learn that the rules are for show. A pause like that would teach everyone that promises about AI safety are theater.

## Where we stand

We are confident a full worldwide pause will not arrive soon. Our own series of essays from this summer, Graduated Obligation (https://humanityandai.com/thismachine/a-proposal-not-a-verdict/), made the same call. The fourth of its six public predictions, to be graded in public in August 2027, is "The coordinated pause will not materialize." The series named that one the prediction it was surest of. OpenAI's two slowdowns do not change that view. Each was one company going slower by its own choice, which is not a coordinated pause. And the industry's most prominent advocate of pacing, Amodei, agrees that a full pause is unlikely any time soon.

So we do not wait for it. Here is what we think would help, in plain terms.

**One. Support slowing down, but only with an inspector who holds all three powers.** A company that slows down with an outside checker who can enter, halt and publish is doing something real. A company that slows down on its own word is doing something good that it can undo tomorrow. This builds on what we said in June. Then, we asked for a slower pace, one that matches our ability to understand these systems. Now we add the condition that makes a slower pace mean something.

**Two. Let the companies talk about safety, in public only.** Congress should write into law the narrow antitrust waiver Amodei asks for, with two conditions attached. Any safety agreement between companies must be published in full, and it must come with an outside inspector. A secret agreement between competitors is exactly what antitrust law was written to stop, and it should stay illegal. The people who enforce that law are wary of the request too. The Federal Trade Commission is one of the two federal agencies that enforce antitrust law. In September, its chairman, Andrew Ferguson, said, speaking for himself, that when companies come to Washington "asking for a host of regulations and an antitrust exemption, all of my alarm bells go off." The news agency Reuters, which reported his remarks, also cited a report by the news service Bloomberg that OpenAI's top lobbyist, Chris Lehane, said his company had been working with Anthropic and Google on AI safety for several weeks and saw no need for an antitrust waiver for the three companies to talk about safety. We read both as reasons to attach the conditions. If rivals coordinate on safety, it should happen where everyone can see it. An everyday picture: neighbors can agree on a shared fence line, but the agreement gets filed at the county courthouse where anyone can read it.

**Three. Put the power to halt in public hands, at more than one level, and make every holder of it answerable.** A single national agency with the power to stop an industry is a great deal of power in one place. So the power to halt should be shared among a national body, the states, and the local communities that host data centers, the buildings full of computers where these systems are built. The attorneys general have already asked for full authority to enforce federal protections, and for no federal override of state laws. Every holder of the power to halt should publish its decisions, name who set each danger line and how that line can be changed, and be removable through ordinary elections and lawmaking. Each country and region will build this its own way.

On October 16, the news outlet Politico reported that Anthropic has quietly pitched lawmakers a draft bill under which the government could halt an AI model only one way, through one court. On October 19, the Daily Caller, a Washington news site, published a 37-page copy of it, marked "Discussion Draft" and "Last edited 9/29/26." Anthropic has not confirmed that copy, and Anthony Cimino, its head of federal affairs, wrote on October 18 that the draft has been revised since. No member of Congress had sponsored it as of October 19. So we read the copy as an early version, not the final word.

In that copy, there is one door to a halt. The head of the Commerce Department, the federal department that oversees trade and industry, can ask a federal court in Washington, D.C., to suspend an AI model's development, its release, or its use inside the company. The bar is high: a present or impending risk that a single incident involving the model will help cause the deaths of, or serious injuries to, more than 50 people, or more than $1 billion in property damage. The risk must come from the model helping someone make a biological weapon, escaping the control of its developer or user, carrying out cyberattacks, or doing AI research in a way that makes one of those three worse. No other path to a halt exists in the bill, though powers under other laws stay as they are. The copy would also stop states from setting their own rules on risk disclosure, outside evaluation and incident reporting.

Measured against the three powers, the copy goes part of the way. The right to enter: the company must give outside evaluators access comparable to its own internal risk teams, but an evaluator has "no authority" to force the company to hand anything over. The right to halt: an evaluator cannot stop anything; only Commerce, through the court, can. The duty to publish: an evaluator must publish a version of its report within 30 days. The company may black out some material first, but must give a written reason, and the evaluator must say in public whether it disagreed. The company chooses and pays its own evaluators, though no payment may depend on what they find. In the restaurant picture, this inspector can read the kitchen's records and post a grade, but cannot close the kitchen. Only a judge can, when the government asks, and only when people are about to be badly hurt. By the test we set above, that is not yet an outside inspector.

Where we would share the power to halt among answerable holders at several levels, this copy puts it behind one door with a high bar, and its outside checkers can look and publish but cannot stop the work. We think sharing is the safer answer.

**Four. Keep building what does not depend on a pause.** That means publicly owned AI strong enough to watch the others, small AI systems that households and towns can run on their own machines, and state laws that protect people now. These help whether a pause comes or not.

**Five. Make caution affordable.** Much of the pressure to race is fear of falling behind, and much of that fear is about jobs and money. The Federal Reserve, the country's central bank, heard it from a business contact in September: "Without data centers, construction would be in a recession." And 71 percent of American adults told the Pew Research Center, a nonpartisan polling organization, this summer that AI will mean fewer jobs over the next twenty years. Put simply, people fear losing work if the building goes on, and fear losing work if it stops. The larger plan we work on, which we call Foundation (https://humanityandai.com/foundation/), starts from a guaranteed floor of basics for every person: food, housing, healthcare, income. A country that guarantees the basics can afford to slow down, because slowing down no longer threatens anyone's rent.

## When we will check, and what we will report

We are not putting odds on any of this. We are making a promise instead.

On Monday, March 29, 2027, we will publish answers to four questions on this page, whatever the answers are:

1. Has any outside inspector anywhere, at any level from a county to a country, gained all three powers over a frontier AI company: to enter, to halt and to publish?
2. Has any embedded evaluator, including Accenture and Faculty at Anthropic, or any outside auditor named under the September 29 White House pledge, published a finding the company did not want published, or reported being refused access?
3. Has Congress passed an antitrust waiver for AI safety agreements, and if so, does it require the agreements to be public?
4. Has the Sanders and Casar bill (S. 5493 in the Senate, H.R. 10538 in the House), or any other pause bill, moved out of committee, the review stage a bill normally clears before the full chamber votes on it?

If the answers to the first two are yes, voluntary pacing is doing more than we expect, and we will say so in those words. If the first answer is still no, then no pause anywhere can be checked by anyone outside, and this position stands. The essay series still grades all six of its predictions in August 2027, on schedule.

## We must pass the same test

We are asking the AI companies to accept a checker they did not choose. Anyone who asks that of others should accept it too. Our proposals should answer to people outside the circle of those who wrote them, in public, and change when the evidence says they should. That is part of this position, not something added at the end.
