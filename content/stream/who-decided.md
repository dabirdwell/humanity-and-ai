On June 9 of this year, you could open a browser and use the most capable artificial intelligence system that had ever been handed to the general public. On June 12, at 5:21 in the evening on a Friday, it was gone. On July 1, it came back.

Nobody in the United States government has ever explained to you, in public, why either of those things happened.

I want to be careful about what I am claiming, because I first wrote a version of this piece in late June while the models were still dark, and that version was angrier than it was useful. The models came back. That is real. If you read this as a man refusing to take yes for an answer, you will have misread it.

Here is what I cannot get past. The decision to take the capability away was made in a letter the public has never seen. The decision to give it back was made in a letter that reached the public because a reporter obtained it, not because the government published it. In between, four members of Congress asked in writing for the legal reasoning, set a deadline, and the deadline passed with no public answer. Then the whole thing was over.

The capability returned. The process that removed it is exactly as unreadable today as it was on the Friday it was used. And a process you cannot read is not made accountable by producing an outcome you happen to like.

**Four terms, and then we can go.**

This next part has some Washington vocabulary in it. None of it is hard, and you do not need to memorize any of it. You only need four things.

**Anthropic** is an American AI company, based in San Francisco. It makes the Claude family of AI models. It is one of a handful of labs, along with OpenAI and Google, that build systems at the very front edge of what the technology can do.

**Fable 5** is a model Anthropic released to the public on June 9, 2026. Public means you. Anyone with an account could use it.

**Mythos 5** is the same underlying system with some of its safety guardrails deliberately removed, so that cybersecurity professionals can use it to hunt for flaws in software. It has never been public. It goes to a small set of vetted organizations.

An **export control** is a rule that says an American company cannot send a particular thing to particular people outside the country without government permission. It was built for physical goods. Missile parts. Advanced computer chips. The machinery is old, and until this June nobody had ever pointed it at a chatbot.

That is the whole vocabulary. Now the timeline, and every date in it comes from a document I read myself, with the source named.

**What actually happened, with dates.**

On June 9, 2026, Anthropic released Fable 5 to the public and made Mythos 5 available to the cybersecurity partners who already had access to an earlier version of it. That is Anthropic's own launch announcement.

On June 12, 2026, at 5:21 p.m. Eastern, the U.S. government sent Anthropic what the export-control world calls an "is informed" letter. Anthropic published a statement the same day describing it. In the company's words, the government, "citing national security authorities, has issued an export control directive to suspend all access to Fable 5 and Mythos 5 by any foreign national, whether inside or outside the United States." That last clause is the part that mattered operationally. It covered foreign nationals standing in the United States, including Anthropic's own non-American employees. There is no way to check a person's citizenship at the instant they type a question into a chat window, so Anthropic did the only thing available and shut both models off for everybody, everywhere.

The stated trigger was a jailbreak, which is the term for getting a model to do something its guardrails were built to refuse. Researchers had found a way to make Fable 5 identify weaknesses in software and, in one case, produce code demonstrating an attack. Anthropic reviewed the same technique and said it surfaced "a small number of previously known, minor vulnerabilities" that other publicly available models can also find, and that "we disagree that the finding of a narrow potential jailbreak should be cause for recalling a commercial model deployed to hundreds of millions of people."

On June 18, 2026, four members of the House sent Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick a bipartisan letter. They were Sam Liccardo, a Democrat from California, Jay Obernolte, a Republican from California, Ted Lieu, a Democrat from California, and Scott Franklin, a Republican from Florida. They asked for the Department's legal authorities, its technical evaluation, its review process, and its criteria for restoring access. They wrote that the action "appears to represent a significant new application of export control authorities to advanced AI systems." They asked for a response by June 26.

On June 25 and 26, the White House asked OpenAI, a different company, to hold its newest model back to a small group of government-approved partners before any wider release. OpenAI agreed on June 26. By multiple accounts this was the first time the U.S. government had preemptively asked an American AI company to restrict a launch before it happened.

On June 26, 2026, the Commerce Department approved a partial return of Mythos 5 to a set of U.S. organizations, reported as mostly companies and agencies that operate and defend critical infrastructure. The same June 26 came and went without a public answer to the four members of Congress.

On June 30, 2026, Secretary Lutnick signed a letter to Anthropic's Chief Compute Officer, Tom Brown, withdrawing the license requirement. Anthropic published its own announcement that day, and the sentence is flat and unambiguous: "As of today, June 30, the export controls on Fable 5 and Mythos 5 have been lifted." Anthropic said it had committed to giving the government pre-release access to models with national security implications, to reporting significant jailbreaks quickly, to putting resources into joint research, and to working toward a shared voluntary security and evaluation standard.

On July 1, 2026, Fable 5 came back. Anthropic's words again: available "to users globally on the Claude Platform, Claude.ai, Claude Code, and Claude Cowork."

Eighteen days from the letter to the withdrawal. Nineteen from the shutoff to the restoration.

**What was never published.**

Now hold onto one pattern through the rest of this, because it is the whole argument and everything else is detail.

Every consequential document in that timeline is a private letter.

The June 12 order has never been released. Writing at Just Security on June 15, Brian Egan, a lawyer who has worked on these authorities, put it plainly: "The U.S. government has not publicized the order that it issued to Anthropic." He noted that Commerce uses "is informed" letters fairly regularly against semiconductor exporters, and that "these letters, which the U.S. government does not make public, are often precursors to broader, public export restrictions."

Nothing was filed in the Federal Register, which is the daily public record where the government is normally required to print the rules it makes. Writing in The Conversation on July 21, João Marinotti, a law professor at Indiana University, noted that the directive skipped a mechanism called a temporary classification, the ordinary path for a fast emergency control, which requires sign-off from the Defense and State Departments, publication, and automatic expiration after a year. Instead the action was unilateral, undisclosed, and open-ended.

The June 30 withdrawal letter became public because Politico obtained a copy, and an image of it circulated on social media. Not because Commerce announced it. Commerce and its Bureau of Industry and Security did not respond to press requests for comment on the withdrawal.

And the four questions from Congress, the legal authority, the technical evaluation, the review process, the criteria, have no public answer that I can find as of today, August 14. The models came back on July 1. The answers did not come back at all.

**The strongest argument against me, stated the way its best advocate would state it.**

Here is the case for the government, and I am not going to make it weak so I can knock it down.

A model that meaningfully lowers the bar for a cyberattack is a genuine danger to real people. Hospitals run on software. So do water systems and payroll and the electrical grid. A credible outside report landed saying this system could be pushed into producing working attack code. When that lands on your desk on a Friday, the responsible move is to stop the bleeding first and litigate the paperwork second. The government did exactly that. It moved in hours. It then spent eighteen days working with the company, got real safety commitments out of it, approved a partial return in fourteen days and a full return in eighteen, and gave the capability back. Nobody was arrested. No company was destroyed. The model is on your screen right now.

And publishing the reasoning would have been actively harmful, because the reasoning is a description of how to break the model. You do not print the exploit. Ask any security professional.

That argument is serious, and the last part of it is the strongest part. I will concede it directly: the specific technical details of a live vulnerability should not be published while it is live.

But that concession costs me nothing, because it is not what anyone asked for. Congress did not ask for the exploit. It asked which statute the Secretary was acting under. That is not a secret. It cannot be a secret. The law is the one part of government that is published by definition. A citizen is entitled to know which power was used on them even when the facts that triggered it stay sealed. Courts do this constantly: sealed evidence, public opinion. The finding stays classified, the authority does not.

And the speed argument proves less than it looks. Fast is a virtue when the mechanism is legitimate. When the mechanism is a private letter with no notice, no hearing, and no appeal, speed is just the same discretion arriving sooner.

**Why getting it back is not the answer.**

Here is the thing that has bothered me since July 1.

The best case anyone can make for this episode is that the system worked. A company built something risky, the government caught it, they negotiated, the government let go. Good outcome.

Except look at what actually did the work. Anthropic complied within hours of a letter it disagreed with, because it had no realistic alternative. Members of Congress asked for the legal basis and were not answered on the record. The reversal came from the same office that imposed the restriction, on its own timetable, by its own judgment, in a document it did not publish. At no point did any of it get tested by anything outside that office.

That is not a system working. That is a single office being reasonable. Those look identical from where you sit, and they are completely different things, because one of them holds when the office is unreasonable and the other one does not.

Secretary Lutnick's withdrawal letter reportedly says he may "reevaluate and adjust the scope of license requirements" should circumstances change, and that the list of approved entities may be amended at any time. Read that sentence as the honest description of your position that it is. The capability you are using right now sits inside somebody's continuing discretion, and the terms of that discretion have not been printed anywhere you can read them.

Ask the practical question. If it happens again next month, to a different company, for a different reason, what exactly is different? What did anyone learn about how the decision gets made? What criteria would let a lab, or a hospital that depends on the tool, or you, see it coming?

Nothing. The answer is nothing. Eighteen days of national attention, four members of Congress, a bipartisan letter, wall-to-wall coverage, and the public record is precisely as empty as it was on June 12.

**What did not come back.**

There is one more piece of this that got lost in the relief.

Fable 5 is fully public again. Mythos 5 is not. As of today, it remains available only to a set of approved organizations, reportedly around a hundred companies and institutions concentrated in critical infrastructure, with expansion decided in consultation with the government. There is no public list of who is on it. There are no published criteria for getting on it.

I am not arguing Mythos should be handed to everybody. It is a tool with its safety guardrails deliberately removed. There is a real argument for keeping it narrow, and I would probably make that argument myself.

I am arguing about who holds the pen and whether anyone can read what they wrote. A government-blessed list of organizations that get the strongest version of a capability, with unpublished criteria and no appeal, is a durable arrangement and not an emergency one. It survived the emergency. It is running right now.

**The development never stopped.**

One more fact, and this one I can source directly to Anthropic's own newsroom rather than to rumor.

The models were dark from June 12 to July 1. During and immediately after that window, Anthropic shipped Claude Sonnet 5 on June 30 and Claude Opus 5 on July 24.

The story we get told about restrictions is that they slow things down. Buy time. Make us safer. Look at what the nineteen days actually did. Frontier training runs take months. The work in the buildings never paused, because it could not pause and did not need to. The only thing that changed on June 12 was what you were allowed to see.

That is the widening, and it is the part I would keep if I had to throw the rest of this away. There is no law, no rule, and no framework in the current arrangement that requires the benefits of the next model to reach you. There is nothing that requires you to be told it exists. The distance between what the powerful can reach and what you can reach does not hold steady while regulators think it over. It grows on its own, as a structural consequence of how the thing is built. Not because anyone chose it. Because nobody built anything to prevent it.

**Why I stopped wanting an IAEA for this.**

Signpost: this next stretch is the governance argument, and it is the most abstract part of the piece. Here is what to hold onto. Everyone is about to tell you the answer is an international agency modeled on the one we built for nuclear weapons. I spent decades believing that. I think it is wrong, for three specific reasons.

The IAEA is the International Atomic Energy Agency, the treaty-based body that has monitored nuclear material since the 1950s. It has mostly worked for seventy years. The instinct to copy it is a good instinct.

First problem. The IAEA was built by countries that already had the bomb, after they had it, and the structure ratifies their position. Build the AI version now and you lock in today's frontier labs and their host governments, mainly the United States and China, as the permanent intelligence powers. Everybody else signs up in the junior tier. That is not a third path. That is the consolidation path wearing a multilateral badge.

Second problem. You can verify a nuclear program because the physics cooperates with you. Enrichment leaves isotopic traces. Reactors emit neutrons. Fissile material has a paper trail written into the atoms. AI is software. You cannot inspect a finished training run and prove what was done in it. The inspection machinery that makes the IAEA function does not transfer, and building an equivalent would either be toothless or would require a surveillance apparatus nobody should want.

Third problem, and this is the one that took me longest to see. The IAEA is a restriction regime with a thin distribution clause bolted onto it. Article IV of the Non-Proliferation Treaty promises peaceful nuclear technology to the countries that sign. In practice the restriction has been strong and the distribution has been weak. Copy that template and you get a body that is excellent at saying who cannot have the capability and useless at making sure the benefits reach anyone. Given everything above, that is the worst available outcome.

**The older analogy.**

The thing I want is Acheson-Lilienthal, and it is 1946.

That is the moment before the consolidation hardened, when the United States, alone in the world with the bomb, sat down and asked itself whether to hold the monopoly forever or institutionalize the transition while it still had the leverage to set the terms. The plan that came out of it failed. The Baruch Plan that followed was read by the Soviets, correctly, as an attempt to freeze American advantage, and the Cold War closed the door on the better version. But notice when the choice got made. During the unilateral window, not after it shut. And the imperfect institution that eventually grew out of that thinking has helped keep the world from nuclear war for seventy years.

We are inside a window like that right now. The United States has the strongest models. China is closing. Open-weight models, the kind you can download and run on your own machine, are pushing capability out into the world where no letter can recall it. The window closes either way. The only question is whether we use it to build something legitimate, or wait until the arrangement is permanent and then try to bolt governance onto a power structure that no longer has to listen.

I see three roads.

The first is consolidation. What is happening now keeps happening. Frontier capability sits with a few companies, gated by governments through processes nobody outside can read, released to approved partners. The gap widens until it is permanent. Call it an intelligence caste system, enforced not by any law but by who can reach what.

The second is the one the AI safety community loses sleep over. Something arrives that is powerful enough that whoever holds it cannot be counterbalanced by any institution, government, or market. I take that seriously. I would only point out that the first road is the slow version of the same destination.

The third is the one worth building. Distributed, governed, accountable. Not one body deciding who gets what, but a framework where frontier capability is held in trust, the rules are made in the open, the benefits flow to the public as a condition of the restriction rather than as a favor, and the people making the calls answer to the people they affect. That last condition is the hard one. It is also the line between governance and feudalism.

I am not arrogant enough to think I have the design. But this June told us three things that framework has to have, because it showed us exactly what happens without them.

It needs a statutory basis. A law Congress passed, not an authority written for hardware in 2018 and pointed at a chat window in 2026.

It needs transparency by default. When a capability is withdrawn, the authority and the criteria get published even if the technical finding stays sealed. Sealed evidence, public opinion. We already know how to do this.

And it needs a positive distribution mandate. If capability is held in trust, the trust has obligations running the other way. Infrastructure defense, public health, research, civic participation. Those flow to the public because the restriction obligates it, not because a company chooses to be generous this quarter.

**The question.**

In June, the strongest AI a member of the public could reach was switched off by a letter sent at 5:21 on a Friday, under an authority the government has not named in public. Eighteen days later the same office switched it back on, in a letter the public saw because a reporter got a copy. Four members of Congress asked, in writing, on the record, what law was used. As of today, they have no public answer. In the middle of it, the strongest version of that same system was put behind a list of approved organizations that is still not published, and that list is still running.

You were not consulted at any point in that. Not when it was taken. Not when it was given back. Not about who is on the list.

So the question I asked in June is not retired by the fact that things worked out. It is the same question, and the reversal made it sharper rather than softer, because now we have watched the discretion run in both directions and we still cannot see the mechanism either time.

Who decided you couldn't have it, and when did they ask you.

**What I could not verify.**

Three things in this piece rest on reporting rather than on a document I read.

I have not read the June 12 order. Nobody outside the government and Anthropic has. Everything above about its contents comes from Anthropic's own June 12 statement and from lawyers analyzing it.

I have not read the June 30 withdrawal letter in an official government version. It reached the public through Politico and through an image posted on social media. The quotations I attribute to it are marked as reported, and I could not find it published by Commerce or the Bureau of Industry and Security.

And I cannot prove a negative about the answer to Congress. What I can say precisely is this: I searched for a public Commerce response to the June 18 letter and did not find one as of August 14, and the members' June 26 deadline passed without one becoming public. Congressman Liccardo's own press pages returned an access error to me directly, so my account of the letter's contents comes from the reporting on it and from quotations of it, not from the PDF. If Commerce answered privately, that is possible and I would not know. It would also make the point rather than refute it.

**How this was made, and what I have at stake.**

I thought this piece through with several Claude models across two drafts, six weeks apart. One of them told me the framing I had been leaning on flattered itself. One corrected my history. One pushed me off a position I had held for decades. Some of the older thinking underneath this got help from Fable, before it was pulled and again after it came back.

I am telling you that because it is the argument, not despite it. The question of who gets to think with these systems, who gets a working partnership with the strongest minds we have built, is the same question this whole piece is about. The future I want is the one where what I just did is available to you. Where the strongest thing there is is something you can actually reach, and not something held behind a list while a smaller version answers your questions.

That is not the default. It has to be built, and the eighteen days in June were a demonstration of how quickly the other outcome can be arranged by people who never have to explain themselves.
