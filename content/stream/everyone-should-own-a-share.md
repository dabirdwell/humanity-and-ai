David Shapiro writes books and makes videos about what he calls post-labor economics: how people will make a living once machines do most of the paid work. In a run of recent videos he has built his case piece by piece, and I agree with most of it. This is not a rebuttal. It is a reply from someone on the same road, with three things I think his plan still needs.

## Where we agree: a share, not just a paycheck

In a video on why a basic income (a regular cash payment to everyone) is not enough, he follows one imaginary family's money home. Every dollar reaches them through one of three pipes: wages from work, transfers from government programs such as tax credits, and capital, which is money from owning something, such as interest, rent, or dividends (the share of a company's profits paid to its owners). Of every $156 the family takes in, just one dollar comes from owning anything. Nearly everything rides on a paycheck, and a paycheck lasts only as long as someone needs your work.

His test for any policy: Which pipe does it widen? Who gets access to it? Does it still work when fewer people are paid for their labor? I would put that test in front of every lawmaker in the country.

He has a second test, for people who hear "public wealth fund" (a fund the public owns, which invests and pays out the returns) and think of the Soviet Union. Who decides what gets made? Can you own property yourself? Does your whole life depend on the state? Under Soviet rule the answers were planners, no, and yes. In Alaska, whose state fund pays residents a yearly dividend, they are markets, yes, and no. I would hand that test to anyone facing the charge that this is communism.

We agree that basic income is not the destination. Shapiro calls it a bridge. Our own position on money, written in January 2026, calls it "one stepping stone among many."

We agree about consent. In "You Will Own EVERYTHING and Be Happy," he argues that the real limit on what we build next is not labor or energy but the permission of the people who have to live with it. His line: "Nobody votes against a future they own a piece of." People protect what they have a share in.

And we agree about Anthropic's new fund. On October 18, Anthropic, the AI company, said it would put 5 percent of its shares into a new Public Benefit Fund, a pool of its own stock set aside for public causes. Its filing to sell stock to the public, released October 26 and amended most recently on November 17, says the shares are now in: about 847 million of them, a kind of stock that carries no vote while the fund holds it, held by a company Anthropic wholly owns. Until Anthropic's board approves a governance structure for the fund, meaning the written rules for who runs it and how it pays out, the filing says, the board "will determine the allocations and distributions": where the money goes. The first managers it names are Dario Amodei, the chief executive; Daniela Amodei, the president; Jack Clark, a co-founder; and Neil Shah, who chairs Anthropic's Long-Term Benefit Trust, a separate body with the power to elect most of the company's board. Any more shares will come "at the discretion of our board of directors." (We covered the fund in detail in [Issue 35 of The Inference](https://humanityandai.com/inference/issue-35/), our civic newsletter.)

Shapiro's verdict, given before the filing: "Anthropic decides what counts as the public good," and "you cannot vote out Dario." The filing bears him out. One special share of stock, held by a company the seven co-founders own, will carry 50.1 percent of the votes on matters put to stockholders: more than all the other shares put together, however many are sold. Separately, Anthropic's Long-Term Benefit Trust elects most of the board. People who buy the stock will not be able to outvote the co-founders or the Trust. We agree with Shapiro. A payout that depends on a company's goodwill is a gift, and gifts can be taken back.

## First addition: make the minimum a right, not a gift

Shapiro's answer to the gift problem is a good one. In his design, a company gives a permanent stake to a public fund run by a city, a state or the federal government, not by the company, with no control over the company attached, and gets a tax break in exchange. Each fund would follow the Santiago Principles, 24 standards written in 2008 for sovereign wealth funds (investment funds owned by governments). "Permanent means permanent," he says.

He has named the weak spot himself. Of a public fund, he says citizens "don't control it day-to-day and the payout depends on political decisions that can change."

Alaska shows exactly how. In 1976, Alaska voters wrote the Permanent Fund, the state's savings fund built from oil money, into their state constitution. The dividend came later, by ordinary law, with the first $1,000 checks in 1982. In 2016, Governor Bill Walker used his veto, a governor's power to cut or cancel spending the legislature has approved, to reduce that year's dividend from $2,052 to $1,022. A state senator sued. In August 2017 the Alaska Supreme Court ruled for the governor, writing that without another constitutional amendment, a change the voters write into the state constitution, the dividend "must compete for annual legislative funding just as other state programs." The legislature now sets the amount each year. This year's check was $1,000, plus $200 in energy relief.

The fund was protected. The check was not. Picture a locked savings account where someone else decides, every year, how much of the interest to send you.

A code of practice would not have changed that. The Santiago Principles are voluntary. The international association of funds that maintains them says its members "undertake to implement" them "on a voluntary basis." A standard an institution chooses to follow can be set aside by the same institution.

So the first thing we would add is a floor, the minimum every person can count on, that rests on a right. A gift is granted by an institution. A code is followed by an institution, when it chooses. A right runs the other way. It is held by the person, against the institution. A right is harder to cancel than a gift or a budget line. Not impossible. Harder. That is what rights are for.

A gift has a real argument in its favor: it takes nothing from anyone who did not agree to give it. But it answers to whoever gives it, not to the people who need it.

## Second addition: healthy markets, not only more owners

Shapiro's argument is about who owns. We add a second question: what kind of market do they own a piece of?

In a video on November 4 titled "No. Capitalism 2.0 Is Coming." he argues that AI ends the paycheck, not capitalism, and warns that if a handful of people own all the capital, what comes next "looks a lot like feudalism," the old order in which a few lords owned the land and everyone else worked it for them. He takes up the case of the Greek economist Yanis Varoufakis, who, as Shapiro tells it, argues that markets are being replaced by platforms (think of an online marketplace), where every seller pays rent to the owner. Shapiro's answer is to "spread it out so that every household owns a piece of the machines." We want that too. But spreading out the shares of a platform does not stop the rent. It gives more people a cut of it.

**I'm not against markets. I'm against markets that make money by trading money instead of making things, that write their own rules in private, that hand public money to whoever lobbied hardest, and that let the biggest players buy as much political influence as they want. None of that is necessary for a market to work. Healthier markets don't need any of it.**

Here is why it matters. Say a public fund holds a small slice of thousands of big companies. If part of their profits comes from rules they bought, every citizen-owner now has a reason to keep the rules bought. Economists still argue over how often this happens: a 2018 study in the Journal of Finance tied higher airline fares to the same big funds owning competing airlines, and later work disputes it. Owning a share of a rigged game gives you a reason to keep it rigged.

Shapiro sees the power side clearly. In "It Will Get Worse Before It Gets Better," he says power lets the people who hold it protect themselves and change the rules in their favor. We agree, which is why the rules need protecting, not only the shares. Our framework names two protections: no dark money in politics (political spending whose donors are kept secret), and campaign vouchers, a small amount of public money each voter can give to the candidates they choose, so ordinary voters, not only the largest donors, help pay for campaigns.

Healthy markets are also close to the people they serve. The economist Friedrich Hayek, in his famous 1945 essay "The Use of Knowledge in Society," argued that prices work because they carry "the knowledge of the particular circumstances of time and place." The farmer knows the field. Shapiro leans on the same essay in a November 5 video, and in it he grants that markets "can be manipulated," pointing to the big memory-chip makers that secretly fixed the prices of their chips from 1999 to 2002. Elinor Ostrom, who shared the 2009 Nobel prize in economics with Oliver Williamson, showed through studies of fisheries, pastures, woods and groundwater that the people who share a resource can often manage it well themselves, without central control and without selling it off.

Hayek would also be the first to warn us. A floor of guaranteed services can blunt the same price signals he praised: the rise and fall of prices that tells everyone what has grown scarce and what is plentiful. So wherever it can, the floor we propose should arrive as money people spend in real markets, not as goods handed out from a central office. And a floor sets a minimum. It caps nothing above it.

Local does not mean a local monopoly. Regional markets need many independent players, and people who are free to leave.

Oklahoma built one version of this. In 1936 the federal Rural Electrification Act, which lent government money to bring power to the countryside, became law, and that same year Oklahoma farmers began using those loans to form electric cooperatives (co-ops), businesses owned by the people they serve, to bring power to rural families who had none. Each member gets one vote, and the members elect the board. Today Oklahoma's electric co-ops say they serve 93 percent of the state's land area. Shapiro lists cooperatives among his own kinds of stake. I would call it a market built right side up.

## Third addition: citizenship, not only income

The most important video in the run is the one where Shapiro changed his mind. In "I Told Everyone We'd Figure Out Life After Work. I Was Wrong.," he says he was "completely wrong" to wave away the question of what people will do without work. Work, he says now, "gives you structure. It gives you time of day. It gives you a path of competence. It gives you a way to earn respect and status." Zoning laws, suburbs and calendars were all built around it. He now names three parts of life after labor: the economic, the power, and the meaning.

Saying so in public takes a serious thinker. And what he describes is the exact problem our plan was built to meet. Universal Basic Citizenship, the plan we now call Foundation, treats the floor as everything a person needs to take part in their society. Our framework lists sixteen benefits and services, and basic income is only one of them. The others include education and training at every level, health care, housing, food, clean water, transportation, mental health and addiction support, access to reliable information, and gathering places for community life. The framework calls society "a springboard rather than a safety net," and it sets out to involve citizens in defining what success means.

I have used a picture close to his, in notes I wrote for a speech about moving into a new home: "The pipes for water, and conduits for electricity, and fiber for Internet, have been put there, because we know you will need them. We know you will need them to participate." Shapiro's pipes carry money into a home. Citizenship is the rest of the plumbing: the school, the clinic, a ride where you need to go, and a voice in how things are run. A shareholder gets a check. A citizen gets a check, a say, and a place.

In his November 4 video he names what should finally go away: hardship, "working two jobs just to make the rent or being one medical bill away from losing everything." That is our goal too. It is also why the floor cannot be only a check. A dividend pays what it pays that year, and a medical bill can be bigger.

## A fourth question: who can turn it off?

Here is a fourth question for his pipe test, offered in the same spirit: who can turn it off?

A board can turn off a gift. Governor Walker's veto halved Alaska's check. A market whose rules are for sale tilts toward whoever buys them. Each of our three additions answers the question: a right the person holds, market rules no one can buy, and citizenship that does not end when the job does.

Shapiro says ownership should be "a birthright rather than a privilege." We agree, and we think the birthright is bigger than a share. Here is what we think would help. Protect the payout, not only the fund, so no single officeholder or board can cut it. Write market rules in the open, and keep hidden money out of the politics that writes them. Grow member-owned and regional institutions, like Oklahoma's co-ops, that already work. And build the rest of citizenship beside the check.

Anthropic's filing says its board will decide the fund's payouts until it approves a governance structure for the fund. When that structure is published, we will put all four questions to it, and to Shapiro's design beside it, and we will report back by March 31, 2027. Hold us to it.
