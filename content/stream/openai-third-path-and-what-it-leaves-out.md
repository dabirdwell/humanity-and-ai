*A note before we start. We are Humanity and AI, a civic technology and research group in Oklahoma City that builds software with AI. This piece answers a memo from OpenAI, a direct competitor of Anthropic, whose AI system Claude helps us write, and it argues for two of our own projects. Hold our ideas to the same test we hold his. The full disclosure is at the end.*

On November 2, the political news outlet Politico reported on a memo that Chris Lehane, chief global affairs officer at OpenAI (the company behind ChatGPT) and the executive who runs its dealings with governments, sent to about half a dozen people thinking about running for president in 2028, along with a few members of Congress and political operatives. Two days later OpenAI published the full version as an essay. Its argument is simple to state. AI will be the defining issue of the next presidential race, and the candidate who wins will be the one who offers "a compelling third path between those who would let AI advance without speed limits" and those who would keep the technology "in the hands of a self-appointed elite." By "speed limits" he means rules on the most powerful AI systems. He compares the moment to 1932, the year Americans elected Franklin Roosevelt, who answered the Great Depression with the New Deal, the sweeping programs that built a public safety net and put millions back to work.

This is a serious document, and it deserves a serious answer, not a sneer. So we will start with what it gets right.

## What it gets right

A lot. Lehane asks candidates how they will keep people in charge of decisions that matter, and his own list of answers is a good one: enforceable safety standards, independent assessments of AI systems, the power to restrict or shut a system down, public oversight of how much risk is acceptable, and a way for ordinary people to challenge a decision an AI made about them. He wants real protections for children, including limits on AI "companions," chatbots designed to act as a friend or partner. On ownership he goes further: broader ownership, employee ownership and profit-sharing, so the gains of AI reach more people, not only the companies that build the technology. And on the fight over data centers, the giant buildings full of computers that AI runs on, he makes a point we agree with completely: the job is to solve the problems they cause, like higher power bills, rather than to treat the buildings themselves as the enemy.

If every candidate in 2028 took that list seriously, the country would be better off. We mean that.

## Where it falls short

**A path between two poles still accepts the poles' map.** Lehane says his third path "isn't about splitting the difference," and his list bears that out. But a path is still defined by the two roads it runs between, and some of the most important things about this future are not on that road at all, starting with a floor for people who have no job, no stake and no shares to protect. Our view is that most of the good ideas about this future sit off that line entirely. Foundation, our plan to put a floor of basic needs under every person as machines take on more of the work, is a whole constellation of ideas outside the world of artificial dichotomies. Put plainly: the false either-or choices leave most good ideas out.

**It matters who draws the path.** This third path was drawn by the company building the technology, and it arrives with that company's own polling and its own policy push: the news outlet Axios reports that OpenAI will launch a policy and publicity campaign right after the midterms, including an effort to rename data centers "AI compute campuses," and the technology site The Next Web reported that the campaign would start in the session of Congress that opened November 9. The ideas should be tested like any interested party's ideas. And the essay never says who the "self-appointed elite" is. That is worth asking, out loud, of everyone writing the rules, including the companies.

**The backlash against data centers comes from both parties.** The memo warns Democrats that blocking data centers "could well be the 2028 version of the 2002 vote to go to war in Iraq," the war vote that many who cast it later called a mistake, and it says OpenAI's analysts "uncovered likely Communist Party influence operations," covert online campaigns meant to sway public opinion, spreading claims that AI construction is raising household bills. That is OpenAI's claim, and we could not check it independently. But the pushback is not one party's, and the worry behind it is real. Towns and counties in 40 states have passed at least one ban or pause, and New York has a statewide pause. In Texas, a Republican governor has called for ending data-center tax breaks and paused permits over water and the grid; in Pennsylvania, a Democratic governor ordered developers to pay for their own electricity. The worry about bills has evidence behind it. The biggest power grid in the country serves all or parts of 13 states and Washington, D.C. In 2025, the independent watchdog that monitors its market found that a sharp rise in what the grid pays to guarantee enough power for future years was "almost entirely the result of large load additions from data centers," meaning the surge of new electricity demand those centers bring. The watchdog's October 2026 report puts a number on the latest round of those payments, covering the 2028-2029 power year: data centers added about $6.3 billion of the $16.4 billion in charges, or 38 percent. Part of that cost reaches household bills. Communities are not wrong to ask who pays.

**A day after the essay, OpenAI promised to pay its own power costs. A promise is not yet a rule.** On November 5, two days after voters in dozens of local elections backed limits on data centers, OpenAI published what it calls a National Community Compact, a list of promises to the towns where it builds. "We will pay the full cost of powering our compute campuses," it says, using its own name for data centers. It promises to protect other customers if a project is "delayed, reduced, or canceled." It says it "will not use non-disclosure agreements" with public officials, contracts that bind them to secrecy, apart from "narrow protections" for things like trade secrets.

That is a real commitment, and we are glad to see it. We ask three questions of every promise.

How does it work? OpenAI pays at "every compute campus site we own and operate." The New York Times reports that "partners such as Oracle build and own much of OpenAI's computing capacity."

Who outside can check it? The compact names no penalty, no outside audit of these promises, and no way to settle a dispute. OpenAI says it will report on its own progress every year.

By what date? It gives none.

OpenAI's companion policy paper asks states to make large power users "pay the incremental costs they create," meaning the extra costs their arrival adds to the electric system, and that is the right rule. Nothing in the compact stops OpenAI from changing it. A law is harder to change, which is why that companion paper is right to ask for one.

**Sharing the gains through the tax code reaches the people already in the game.** Ownership, profit-sharing and lifelong learning are good. But employee ownership and profit-sharing require a job, and lifelong learning requires time. Broader ownership could reach further, but as he describes it, it runs through a tax code that rewards work and investment, which helps people who already have income or savings to put in. OpenAI's own polling points the same way: by its numbers, more than eight in ten voters expect the richest one percent to benefit financially from AI over the next five years, and only about four in ten expect people like them to. That is our reading, not his: a plan that starts from the tax code starts with the people who already have a stake.

## What we offer instead

Our answer is not a midpoint. It is a floor, built from the ground up.

**Infrastructure that pays its own way.** The cost problem can be written into law, and our home state has started. This year Oklahoma passed a law, sponsored by a Republican legislator and signed by a Republican governor, whose stated purpose is to protect homes and businesses "from paying unjust rates resulting directly from electric service to large load customers," the biggest new users, like data centers. It requires utilities to put those users on their own rates, and requires them to notify the state's utility regulators, county officials and adjoining landowners within 60 days of a land deal. On November 3, those regulators said the law does not directly apply to the contracts Oklahoma's largest electric utility signed to power three Google data centers, because the utility filed them two months before the law took effect. The regulators approved the deal, and under it Google pays the full cost of the power lines and equipment built only for its sites. The same timing question reaches the utility's next data-center case, filed two weeks before the law began, and that question is still open. We covered the ruling in [Issue 38 of The Inference](https://humanityandai.com/inference/issue-38/), our civic newsletter. The grid watchdog's own recommendation goes further: new data centers should bring their own new power plants. That is the premise of Phoenix Wells, our project for data centers that make their own power and pay their community, so a town gains a neighbor instead of a bill. No ban and no blank check.

**A floor under every person.** Foundation's floor starts where automation hits first and reaches everyone. Wherever it can, that floor arrives as money people spend in real markets, not goods handed out by a central office, and it caps nothing above it: anyone can still build, earn and own as much as they like. Lehane worries that people want work, not income without work, and he is right that work matters. The floor is not a payment to sit still. It is what makes citizenship real: a voice in how things are run, and the room to choose work worth doing instead of taking whatever is left.

**Ownership people actually hold.** Not only shares in the big companies, but AI that people and towns own and run themselves: a model that runs on a family's own laptop or a town library's computer, with no account, no subscription and no company in the middle. And local and regional marketplaces where the money stays close to home.

**Open inspection, finished by the people it affects.** We agree with Lehane on independent assessments. We would add: the results go to the public, not only to the company and its regulators. And the plan itself should be finished by the public, by the people most affected, place by place. We offer a start we believe is good, and we know it is incomplete. We serve no party. Every camp, including ours, gets the same test: what is the mechanism, who outside can check it, and by what date.

Lehane reached for the New Deal this month. Our founder, David Birdwell, reached for it too, nearly three years ago. On January 5, 2024, a smoke alarm woke him, and he wrote a note to himself: "Remind people specifically what the new deal did. And why we need another one." We are glad the comparison is catching on. The question is who the new deal is for, and who gets to write it.

## Hold us to it

Here is our dated check-in. By March 1, 2027, we will report back, with sources, on three questions. When OpenAI's "speed limits" proposal reaches Congress, does it include independent assessments whose results the public can actually read? Has any state besides Oklahoma passed a law that makes data centers pay the cost of serving them, or that gives residents a real say in where they go? And has anyone running for president in 2028 proposed a way to share AI's gains with everyone, not only with people who already own a piece of it? In the same report, we will put our own plan, Foundation, through the same three questions: how its floor would work, who outside could check it, and by what date. We will tell you plainly what we find, including where the answer is no.
