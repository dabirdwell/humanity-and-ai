In the latest national study, which used 2017 data, the typical American household spent about 3 percent of its income on home energy. The typical low-income household spent about 8 percent, more than twice the share. In plain words: for a family earning $30,000, that is about $200 a month just to keep the lights and heat on.

Energy burden isn't spread evenly. It falls hardest on people in older homes with inefficient heating, cooling and appliances.

## What the thresholds mean

Energy researchers use two lines:

**High burden (6 percent or more):** At this level, energy costs start crowding out other necessities. In a federal survey, many households said they cut back on or went without food or medicine to pay an energy bill.

**Severe burden (10 percent or more):** In 2017, about two in five low-income households across the country paid this much. In the Oklahoma City area, the typical low-income household paid 7.8 percent, and one in four paid more than 12.5 percent.

## Why it's a policy problem

Low-income households on average use less energy than wealthier ones, not more. A big part of the problem is housing: older, leakier homes they can't afford to fix. Few ever get weatherization help. The federal Weatherization Assistance Program fixes about 32,000 homes a year with Energy Department funds, and those homes save $372 or more a year on average.

Foundation's [Sustainable Energy](/foundation/freedom-to/sustainable-energy/) component proposes community-owned energy, with affordable bills as a design goal from the start.
