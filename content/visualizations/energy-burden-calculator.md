The typical American household spends about 3 percent of its income on home energy. The typical low-income household spends about 8 percent, more than twice the share. In plain words: for a family earning $30,000, that is about $200 a month just to keep the lights and heat on.

Energy burden isn't spread evenly. It follows old housing, inefficient appliances, and decades of underinvestment in the neighborhoods least able to absorb the cost.

## What the thresholds mean

Researchers and the U.S. Department of Energy use two lines:

**High burden (6 percent or more):** At this level, energy costs start crowding out other necessities. Households facing high energy burdens report skipping meals, going without medicine, and keeping their homes at unsafe temperatures.

**Severe burden (10 percent or more):** About two in five low-income households across the country pay this much. In the Oklahoma City area, the typical low-income household pays 7.8 percent, and one in four pays more than 12.5 percent.

## Why it's a policy problem

Energy burden isn't mostly about using more energy. Low-income households often use less. But they tend to live in older, leakier homes they can't afford to fix, and few ever get weatherization help: the federal Weatherization Assistance Program fixes about 32,000 homes a year with Energy Department funds. Those homes save $372 or more a year on average.

Foundation's [Sustainable Energy](/foundation/freedom-to/sustainable-energy/) component proposes community-owned energy, with affordable bills as a design goal from the start.
