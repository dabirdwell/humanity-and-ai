## Since 1979, work creates almost twice as much; pay is up about a third

Around 1979, that stopped. From the end of 1979 to the middle of 2026, what an hour of work creates in the American economy rose 93.7 percent. A typical worker's hourly pay, counting benefits and adjusted for inflation, rose 32.7 percent. In plain words: an hour of work now creates almost twice as much as it did in 1979, and the typical paycheck grew by about a third.

The difference did not disappear. According to the Economic Policy Institute, it went into the salaries of highly paid corporate and professional employees, and into higher profits for shareholders and other wealth owners. The shaded area on the chart is that difference.

## Why it happened: not the machines alone

Economists still argue about the causes. The Economic Policy Institute, which keeps these numbers, points to choices made from the late 1970s on: more unemployment was accepted as the price of holding down inflation, raises in the minimum wage became smaller and rarer, labor law did not keep up as employers fought unions harder, tax rates on top incomes were cut, and federal rules for industries such as trucking and airlines were rolled back.

The economists Anna Stansbury and Lawrence Summers argue that the link between work and pay is less broken than it looks: in years when what an hour of work produces grows faster, pay still tends to grow faster too. Something unrelated to how much work produces, they write, has been holding typical pay down, and their results do not strongly support explanations that blame technology alone.

Those sources disagree on some things. Both support this much: the machines alone did not do this. Our reading is that the rules written around the machines made the difference, and rules can be rewritten.

## Why it matters now: AI could widen the gap

AI may raise what an hour of work is worth more than anything before it. If the old pattern holds, those gains will go mostly to whoever owns the machines. That is the problem everything we do is trying to answer.

It exists because of this chart: the idea is to send even a fraction of that shaded gap back to the people whose pay fell behind.
